Why this cures the robotic feel: a long syllable (=) is twice a short (–). Played into dead silence, it is a tap and an empty wait. With a drone and a one-beat tick underneath, the ear hears the long note hold through two ticks: that is the groove. Foot Breath is off by default because reciters do not pause between feet; the only breath is at a caesura.

Poets do not scan by arithmetic; they hear the kasr-e vazn, the limp. Each line below is a real line by Ghalib or Mir. In the stumbling version one syllable of one word has the wrong weight. Listen and pick the word.

How the limp is made: one syllable whose weight is fixed (not a flexible vuh, kih or to, which may go either way) is made long when it should be short, or short when it should be long, and the scanner confirms that no bahr accepts the result. So the stumble is real, and it sits in one word.

Every bahr family has an anchor verse that works as a tuning fork: poets keep it running in their head to check whether a new line is in rhythm.

YOUR TAPS AGAINST THE METER0 taps

Hands first, then ears: tap a long syllable as one beat and a short as half, steady, as Pritchett advises, and say the afāʿīl with it. While the anchor loops, each round of taps is scored: dashed outlines are what the meter wants, filled bars are what you tapped, red where you made a long short or a short long. With the loop off, tap the line from memory and see how your rhythm reads.
